\chapter{Ablation Study and Comparisons}
\label{chap:ablation}

This chapter isolates the contribution of each major component in the proposed framework through systematic ablation experiments. The goal is to determine which design choices are essential for the parkour policy's performance and which provide marginal or no benefit. We first define the baseline configuration and the ablation variants (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_setup}), then present the main ablation results in a unified table (Section~\ref{sec:main_ablation_table}), followed by detailed analyses of each individual ablation (Sections~\ref{sec:ablation_memory_attention}--\ref{sec:ablation_sym_aug}). We then examine training dynamics (Section~\ref{sec:training_dynamics}), reward decomposition (Section~\ref{sec:reward_decomposition}), and termination patterns (Section~\ref{sec:termination_analysis}). Supplementary experiments conducted under different environment scales are reported in Section~\ref{sec:supplementary_experiments}. Finally, we compare with existing training results from the literature (Section~\ref{sec:comparison_existing}) and summarize the findings (Section~\ref{sec:ablation_summary}).

\section{Ablation Setup}
\label{sec:ablation_setup}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Baseline Configuration}
\label{sec:baseline_config}

The baseline (BASE) is the full proposed method described in Chapter~\ref{chap:proposed_method}. It includes all five components: (1)~a Memory-Attention Encoder with GRU-based memory and cross-attention, (2)~an Adversarial Motion Prior (AMP) discriminator that provides style reward, (3)~a depth perception module with temporal-subsampled depth image stack, (4)~a 4-expert Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) actor-critic, and (5)~symmetric data augmentation for the AMP reference motions. Additionally, the baseline includes domain randomization strategies (sensor noise, dynamics randomization, terrain randomization) and a volume penetration shaping reward.

\subsection{Ablation Variants}
\label{sec:ablation_variants}

We define seven ablation variants (A1--A7) for the main comparison group, each removing or modifying a single component from the baseline. All variants are trained with 2048 parallel environments under the same training protocol.

\label{tab:ablation_variants}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}cp{3.5cm}p{7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{ID} & \textbf{Variant Name} & \textbf{Modification} \\
\midrule
A1 & No Memory-Attention & Replace Memory-Attention Encoder with basic Conv2d encoder \\

\textbf{A1 (No Memory-Attention).} The Memory-Attention Encoder is replaced with a standard Conv2d depth encoder that produces only a depth latent vector, removing the recurrent memory state and cross-attention. The algorithm reverts from WasabiMemoryAttentionPPO to WasabiPPO, removing the memory consistency loss.

\textbf{A2 (No AMP).} The AMP discriminator and style reward are removed. The policy is trained with only the task reward (velocity tracking, posture, regularization).

\textbf{A3 (No Depth).} The depth image observation is entirely removed. The policy receives only proprioceptive observations. This eliminates the entire visual perception pipeline.

\textbf{A4 (Single Expert).} The 4-expert MoE is replaced with a single-expert network of the same hidden dimensions [256, 128, 64] without routing.

\textbf{A5 (Low Sensor Randomization).} Sensor noise levels are reduced and the delayed-frame mechanism for depth images is disabled.

\textbf{A6 (No Penetration Shaping).} The volume penetration penalty weight is set to zero.

\textbf{A7 (No Symmetric Augmentation).} The AMP discriminator is trained only on original right-foot-leading clips without mirrored counterparts.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_metrics}

Since the training environment does not include an explicit evaluation phase, we derive proxy metrics from the training logs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Success rate} ($\uparrow$): Approximated by the \texttt{time\_out} termination ratio---the fraction of episodes that reached the maximum length (1000 steps = 20\,s) without early termination.
    \item \textbf{Fall rate} ($\downarrow$): Sum of \texttt{base\_contact}, \texttt{bad\_orientation}, and \texttt{root\_height} termination ratios.
    \item \textbf{Mean episode length} ($\uparrow$): Average steps per episode.
    \item \textbf{Mean task reward} ($\uparrow$): Average episodic return from the task reward group.
    \item \textbf{Terrain out-of-bounds rate} ($\downarrow$): Fraction of episodes terminated for leaving the terrain boundary.
\end{itemize}

All metrics are reported at the final training iteration (29990--29999) to reflect converged performance.

\section{A1: Memory-Attention Encoder}
\label{sec:ablation_memory_attention}
%% ============================================================

The memory-attention encoder is the central architectural contribution of this thesis. A1 replaces it with a standard Conv2d depth encoder, removing both the GRU memory state and the cross-attention output.

\subsection{Quantitative Results}

\begin{table}[t]

\begin{tabular}{lcc}

\midrule
Success rate & \textbf{0.964} & 0.951 \\
Fall rate & \textbf{0.036} & 0.049 \\
Mean episode length & 989 & \textbf{993} \\
Task reward & \textbf{83.09} & 79.83 \\
Track linear velocity & 31.67 & \textbf{31.90} \\
Track angular velocity & \textbf{19.26} & 17.75 \\
Feet air time & \textbf{0.76} & 0.50 \\
Volume penetration & $-$0.83 & $-$0.63 \\
Heading error & $-$5.92 & $-$3.92 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

The success rate decreases by 1.3 pp and fall rate increases by 1.3 pp. These differences are modest but consistent across termination sub-metrics: A1 shows higher bad orientation (0.014 vs.\ 0.009), base contact (0.006 vs.\ 0.005), and root height termination (0.029 vs.\ 0.022).

A1 achieves a slightly higher mean episode length (993 vs.\ 989). This apparent contradiction with the lower success rate is explained by the distribution of episode outcomes: A1's non-timeout episodes tend to terminate later (minimum episode length 513 vs.\ 314 for BASE), raising the mean even though fewer episodes reach maximum length.

\subsection{Behavioral Analysis}

The most revealing difference is in feet air time (0.50 vs.\ 0.76), indicating that A1 lifts its feet less frequently. Without the memory state, A1 reacts to terrain only through the current depth image, limiting its ability to plan foot placements ahead of time. The memory-attention encoder allows the baseline to anticipate upcoming obstacles and prepare stepping motions in advance.

The heading error reward is less negative for A1 ($-$3.92 vs.\ $-$5.92). In the baseline, the policy sometimes deviates from the heading to navigate around obstacles (e.g., taking a wider path around a wall), incurring a higher heading penalty but resulting in more successful navigation. A1's lower heading penalty may reflect a more conservative strategy that avoids obstacle-rich regions rather than actively navigating through them.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The memory-attention encoder provides a moderate but consistent improvement. The memory state enables terrain information accumulation over time, particularly valuable when the depth image does not fully reveal the terrain ahead. The relatively small gap suggests the feedforward depth encoder already captures much of the needed terrain information; the memory-attention mechanism's contribution becomes more significant in scenarios requiring temporal context, as discussed qualitatively in Chapter~\ref{chap:experimental_results}.

A2 removes the AMP discriminator entirely, training with only the task reward.

A2 presents a paradox: the task reward is substantially higher (109.96 vs.\ 83.09), yet success rate is lower and fall rate is higher. This is resolved by examining individual reward terms.

\subsection{Reward Decomposition and Motion Style}

Without the AMP style constraint, the policy discovers strategies that maximize task reward but produce dysfunctional motion:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Shuffling gait.} Feet air time drops dramatically from 0.76 to 0.14. Without AMP encouraging human-like stepping, the policy adopts a shuffling gait that maintains ground contact. This is effective on flat ground (hence high velocity tracking) but fails on obstacles requiring precise foot lifting.
    \item \textbf{Exploitation of stand-still reward.} The stand-still reward switches from $-$3.92 to +7.67, indicating A2 frequently enters states where the velocity command is zero and exploits the stand-still reward by remaining motionless rather than actively navigating.
    \item \textbf{Reduced dynamic agility.} The flatter orientation ($-$0.23 vs.\ $-$3.59) and lower heading error indicate a more conservative, less dynamic style that limits aggressive parkour maneuvers.
\end{enumerate}

The base contact termination rate increases by a factor of 7.6 (0.038 vs.\ 0.005), the primary contributor to A2's higher fall rate. Without AMP's upright posture encouragement, the policy occasionally adopts postures that bring the torso into contact with the ground.

\subsection{Interpretation}

The AMP reward is essential for two reasons: (1)~it prevents exploitation of reward loopholes (shuffling, standing still) that maximize task reward but produce dysfunctional behavior, and (2)~it encourages a dynamic, human-like gait better suited for obstacle traversal. The 2.2 pp success rate reduction confirms that AMP is not merely stylistic but functionally improves task performance.

A3 shows the second-largest degradation: success rate drops by 8.3 pp and fall rate increases by 8.1 pp. The root height termination rate increases by a factor of 4.5 (0.098 vs.\ 0.022), indicating the robot frequently fails to maintain adequate height---likely stepping into gaps or descending stairs without preparation.

\subsection{Terrain-Specific Failure Modes}

Without depth perception, the policy must rely entirely on proprioceptive feedback to react to terrain changes. This reactive strategy fails in several terrain types:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Gaps and holes.} The robot cannot see a gap ahead and steps into it, causing root height to drop below the termination threshold.
    \item \textbf{Stairs and steps.} Without visual preview, the robot cannot prepare appropriate foot lifting height. The low feet air time (0.21 vs.\ 0.76) confirms insufficient foot lifting.
    \item \textbf{Walls.} The heading error is much more negative ($-$10.83 vs.\ $-$5.92), suggesting frequent wall collisions requiring large heading corrections.
    \item \textbf{Volume penetration.} The penetration penalty more than doubles ($-$1.85 vs.\ $-$0.83), confirming frequent leg-obstacle intersections.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Residual Capability}

Despite the significant drop, A3 still achieves 0.881 success rate, indicating proprioception alone supports basic locomotion on simpler terrain. The 88.1\% reflects the fraction of episodes where terrain is sufficiently simple for reactive locomotion. The additional 8.3 pp from depth perception corresponds to episodes where terrain is too complex for reactive strategies.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Depth perception is the second most important component. The 8.3 pp success rate reduction confirms that visual terrain understanding is essential for parkour. The policy cannot reliably navigate complex terrain without seeing it, and proprioceptive feedback alone is insufficient for anticipatory obstacle avoidance.

A7 shows the largest degradation among all main-group ablations: success rate drops by 11.1 pp and fall rate increases by 11.1 pp. The bad orientation termination increases by a factor of 4.1 (0.037 vs.\ 0.009), and root height termination by a factor of 4.6 (0.102 vs.\ 0.022).

\subsection{Lateral Bias Analysis}

The most striking behavioral differences are in heading error and feet air time:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heading error = 0.00}: The robot never deviates from its commanded heading. Combined with the very negative stand-still reward ($-$10.33), this suggests the robot frequently stands still while velocity commands are active, incurring a large stand-still penalty but no heading penalty.
    \item \textbf{Feet air time = 0.00}: The robot never lifts its feet off the ground. The policy has entirely lost the ability to step, instead adopting a shuffling or standing strategy.
\end{itemize}

These behaviors are consistent with a dominant-side bias: the AMP discriminator, trained only on right-foot-leading motions, encourages the policy to always lead with the right foot. When terrain requires left-foot-leading steps, the policy cannot produce the appropriate motion and instead stands still or shuffles.

\subsection{Why Symmetric Augmentation Matters}

The symmetric augmentation strategy addresses a specific data imbalance. All captured motions are performed with the right foot leading, creating unilateral bias in the AMP training data. Without augmentation:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The AMP discriminator learns that only right-foot-leading motions are ``human-like,'' penalizing left-foot-leading motions as ``unnatural.''
    \item The policy avoids left-foot-leading steps to maintain high AMP reward, even when terrain demands them.
    \item On terrain requiring alternating or left-leading steps, the policy fails, leading to falls and early terminations.
\end{enumerate}

The mirrored augmentation resolves this by providing both left-leading and right-leading examples, ensuring the style reward does not bias the policy toward one side.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Symmetric augmentation is the most impactful component, with an 11.1 pp success rate improvement. This highlights a subtle but critical interaction between the AMP module and training data: the AMP reward can inadvertently create behavioral biases if the reference motion dataset is not balanced. The symmetric augmentation strategy is a simple but effective solution that doubles the dataset size and eliminates lateral bias.

\section{Comparison with Existing Training Results}
\label{sec:comparison_existing}
%% ============================================================

We compare the proposed method with existing perceptive locomotion approaches for humanoid robots. Direct numerical comparison is challenging because different methods use different robots, terrains, training protocols, and evaluation metrics. We therefore focus on qualitative comparisons and highlight the architectural differences that our ablation study validates.

\subsection{Comparison with Height-Scanner Methods}

Several existing methods use height scanners (ray-cast terrain profiles) as the primary terrain representation~\cite{zhang2024rpl,cheng2024extremeparkour}. Our A3 ablation (No Depth) demonstrates that removing visual perception entirely reduces success rate by 8.3 pp, confirming that terrain information is essential. However, the height scanner provides only a 1D terrain profile beneath the robot, while our depth image stack provides a 2.5D representation with temporal context. The memory-attention encoder further enhances this representation by accumulating terrain information over time.

The A1 ablation (No Memory-Attention) shows that replacing the memory-attention encoder with a basic depth encoder reduces success rate by 1.3 pp. This improvement, while modest, represents the additional value of temporal accumulation and spatial attention beyond what a single-frame depth representation provides. Height-scanner methods lack both temporal accumulation and spatial attention, suggesting that they would benefit even more from the memory-attention mechanism.

\subsection{Comparison with Single-Frame Depth Methods}

Recent methods use single-frame depth images processed through a convolutional encoder~\cite{zhu2024hiking,zhuang2024humanoidparkour}. Our A1 ablation approximates this approach: the basic Conv2d encoder processes depth images without temporal memory or attention. The 1.3 pp success rate reduction confirms that the memory-attention mechanism provides additional value beyond single-frame depth processing.

The key difference is temporal context: single-frame methods cannot distinguish whether an obstacle is approaching or receding, nor can they accumulate terrain information over time. The GRU memory module addresses both limitations, while the cross-attention mechanism provides spatial focus that single-frame methods lack.

\subsection{Comparison with AMP-Based Methods}

AMP-based locomotion methods~\cite{peng2021amp,cheng2024extremeparkour} use the adversarial motion prior to produce natural-looking motions. Our A2 ablation confirms that AMP is essential for preventing reward exploitation and maintaining motion quality. The 2.2 pp success rate reduction when removing AMP demonstrates that the style reward is not merely aesthetic but functionally important.

Our A7 ablation reveals a previously unreported interaction between AMP and training data: the AMP discriminator can create lateral bias if the reference motions are not symmetrically augmented. The 11.1 pp success rate reduction from removing symmetric augmentation is the largest among all ablations, highlighting the importance of balanced training data for AMP-based methods.

\subsection{Comparison with MoE Methods}

Mixture-of-experts architectures have been used for multi-task locomotion~\cite{cheng2024extremeparkour}. Our A4 ablation confirms that the MoE routing mechanism provides a measurable benefit (1.4 pp) by enabling expert specialization across terrain types. The routing visualization (Figure~\ref{fig:moe_routing}) shows that the baseline learns meaningful terrain-dependent expert assignments, which a single-expert network cannot replicate.

\subsection{Key Findings}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Symmetric augmentation is the most critical component.} The 11.1 pp success rate reduction when removing it is the largest among all ablations. This result reveals a previously underappreciated interaction between AMP and training data: the AMP discriminator can create behavioral biases if the reference motions are not balanced. The symmetric augmentation strategy is a simple but essential fix.

    \item \textbf{Depth perception is essential for parkour.} The 8.3 pp reduction confirms that visual terrain understanding is necessary for complex terrain navigation. Proprioception alone supports basic locomotion (88.1\% success) but fails on terrain requiring anticipatory obstacle avoidance.

    \item \textbf{AMP is a functional regularizer, not merely a style enhancement.} The 2.2 pp reduction demonstrates that AMP prevents reward exploitation (shuffling, standing still) and encourages a dynamic gait better suited for obstacle traversal. The higher task reward without AMP (109.96 vs.\ 83.09) is misleading---it reflects aggressive but unstable strategies.

    \item \textbf{MoE and memory-attention provide moderate but consistent improvements.} Both contribute approximately 1.3--1.4 pp to success rate. The MoE benefit comes from expert specialization across terrain types, while the memory-attention benefit comes from temporal terrain context accumulation.

    \item \textbf{Sensor randomization and penetration shaping have small simulation effects.} Their simulation improvements (1.3 pp and 0.6 pp) are modest, but they serve critical sim-to-real transfer functions that cannot be measured in simulation alone.

    \item \textbf{The baseline achieves the best balance across all metrics.} No single ablation outperforms the baseline on success rate or fall rate, confirming that all components contribute positively to the overall system.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Design Implications}

The ablation results suggest several design principles for perceptive parkour policies:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Balance AMP training data.} When using AMP with motion capture data, symmetric augmentation is essential to prevent lateral bias. This simple strategy can be applied to any AMP-based method.
    \item \textbf{Invest in depth perception.} Visual terrain understanding provides the largest marginal improvement over proprioception-only policies. The specific depth representation (single frame, temporal stack, or memory-augmented) has a smaller but still positive effect.
    \item \textbf{Use AMP as a regularizer.} The AMP reward should be viewed not as a style enhancement but as a functional component that prevents reward exploitation and improves task performance.
    \item \textbf{Consider MoE for multi-terrain tasks.} The MoE architecture provides a measurable improvement by enabling expert specialization, particularly when the task requires qualitatively different locomotion strategies across terrain types.
    \item \textbf{Apply sensor randomization for deployment.} While the simulation-only effect is modest, sensor randomization is essential for sim-to-real transfer and provides a small training regularization benefit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

This ablation study has several limitations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Single-run results.} Each ablation variant is trained once, without multiple random seeds. The reported metrics may vary across runs, and the relative rankings could change with different random initializations. However, the large gaps for A3 (8.3 pp) and A7 (11.1 pp) are unlikely to be reversed by random variation.
    \item \textbf{Proxy metrics.} The success rate is approximated by the time-out termination ratio, which does not distinguish between ``the robot successfully navigated the terrain'' and ``the robot survived but did not reach the target.'' A dedicated evaluation protocol with explicit success criteria would provide more precise metrics.
    \item \textbf{Environment scale confound.} The supplementary variants (A8--A10) use 256 environments, making direct comparison with the 2048-environment baseline impossible. A controlled experiment varying only the environment count would isolate the scale effect.
    \item \textbf{No real-robot validation of ablations.} The ablation results are entirely simulation-based. Components with small simulation effects (sensor randomization, penetration shaping) may have larger real-robot effects that cannot be measured in simulation.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Future Directions}

The ablation results suggest several promising directions for future work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Multi-seed ablation.} Training each variant with 3--5 random seeds would provide confidence intervals and enable statistical significance testing.
    \item \textbf{Terrain-specific ablation.} Evaluating each variant on individual terrain types (stairs, gaps, walls) would reveal which components are most important for specific terrain challenges.
    \item \textbf{Sim-to-real ablation.} Deploying ablated policies on real hardware would measure the real-world importance of sensor randomization and penetration shaping.
    \item \textbf{Memory-attention architecture search.} The moderate improvement from the memory-attention encoder (1.3 pp) suggests that alternative memory architectures (e.g., transformer-based memory, larger GRU hidden states) might provide larger improvements.
\end{itemize}
